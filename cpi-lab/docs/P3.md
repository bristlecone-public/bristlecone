# P3 — item-level inflation persistence

`pipelines/p3_persistence.py` → tables `p3_persist`, `p3_index`, output `$CPI_ROOT/out/p3_persistence.json`.
Run: `cd $CPI_ROOT && ./p3_persistence.py` (~35 s end to end). `--help` prints the full spec.

---

## Part 1 — plain language

### The question

Headline CPI is an average of about 180 individual things. Some of them change price
constantly — gasoline, tomatoes, airline fares. Some of them move in slow, long steps —
rent, insurance premiums, restaurant menus. When something pushes a price up, how long
does that push stay in the item's inflation rate?

P3 measures that for every item, every month, and then asks the question people actually
care about: **is today's inflation concentrated in the fast-moving stuff (likely to fade
on its own) or in the slow-moving stuff (likely to still be here next year)?**

### What a half-life means

For each item we ask: *if this month's inflation rate is 1 percentage point above the
item's average, how much of that is still there next month?* Call that fraction **rho**.
If rho is 0.85, then 85% of this month's deviation carries into next month, 72% into the
month after, and so on. The **half-life** is how many months it takes for half the shock
to disappear:

| rho | half-life |
|-----|-----------|
| 0.9 | 6.6 months |
| 0.85 | 4.3 months |
| 0.7 | 1.9 months |
| 0.5 | 1.0 month |
| 0 or below | 0 — the deviation is gone the following month |

A half-life of 0 does not mean the item's prices never move. It means its *monthly rate*
has no memory: a hot month tells you nothing about next month. Gasoline behaves that way.
A half-life of 4 months means shocks visibly linger.

Half-lives here are short by everyday intuition. The stickiest seasonally-adjusted item in
the basket, owners' equivalent rent, has a half-life of about **4.4 months**. That is not a
statement that rents reset every four months — it is a statement about how quickly a
deviation in the *rate of change* of the rent index dies out. Levels are enormously
persistent; rates of change are much less so.

### Sticky vs flexible CPI

We rebuild the headline index twice, using the same December-chained Laspeyres arithmetic
as the official replica, but tilting the weights:

* **sticky CPI** — each item's spending weight multiplied by how persistent it is
* **flexible CPI** — each item's spending weight multiplied by how *un*-persistent it is

Both cover the whole basket; they just look at it from opposite ends. Sticky inflation
running hot is more worrying than flexible inflation running hot, because by construction
the sticky component takes longer to unwind.

### Why owners' equivalent rent dominates the sticky index

Two reasons multiply together:

1. OER is **25.2%** of the CPI basket on its own — a quarter of everything, before any
   persistence adjustment.
2. It also has the **highest** persistence of any seasonally-adjusted item (rho 0.854).

Weight × persistence puts OER at **33.4%** of the sticky index's total weight, with rent of
primary residence adding another 10.5%. **Shelter alone is about 44% of the sticky CPI.**
So "sticky inflation" in this pipeline is, to a first approximation, a shelter index with
a tail of vehicles and restaurants attached. Read every sticky-CPI movement with that in
mind; it is not an independent read on the economy.

Top eight of the sticky basket (weight year 2025, % of the sticky index's weight):

| item | share |
|------|-------|
| Owners' equivalent rent of primary residence | 33.4 |
| Rent of primary residence | 10.5 |
| New vehicles | 5.0 |
| Used cars and trucks | 3.5 |
| Limited service meals and snacks | 3.4 |
| Motor vehicle insurance | 3.4 |
| Gasoline (all types) | 3.3 |
| Full service meals and snacks | 3.1 |

The flexible basket is much flatter — hospital services 7.4%, electricity 3.2%,
prescription drugs 2.6%, and a long tail.

### What it says right now (2026-07)

* Sticky CPI **+3.30%** year over year, flexible CPI **+3.30%**. The spread is **+0.01 pp**:
  the current heat is spread almost perfectly evenly across persistent and transitory
  items. That is unusual — the spread was +0.87 pp a year ago and swung to −0.74 pp in
  February 2026.
* Of the seasonally-adjusted basket (86% of CPI-U), **61.6% of weight sits in items with a
  half-life under 3 months** and **38.4% between 3 and 12 months**. No SA item has a
  half-life above 12 months.
* Stickiest SA items: owners' equivalent rent (4.4 m), rent of primary residence (4.2 m),
  new vehicles (1.8 m), airline fares (1.4 m), used cars (1.1 m).
* Most flexible SA items by weight: hospital services, other miscellaneous foods, pet
  services, services by other medical professionals — all with rho at or below zero.
* OER's current half-life sits at the **73rd percentile of its own 2010-2026 history**: it
  is more persistent than usual, but well below the 2024 peak (rho 0.899, half-life 6.5 m).

### Reading the Atlanta Fed comparison

The Atlanta Fed publishes a sticky-price CPI too, and ours does not match it. That is
expected and is the most useful thing in this pipeline to understand.

* **Theirs** splits the basket by *how often prices change* (Bils–Klenow micro evidence:
  an item is "sticky" if its price is reset less often than every 4.3 months). It is a
  fact about price-setting.
* **Ours** splits by *how persistent the published index's inflation rate is*. It is a fact
  about the time series.

The classifications genuinely disagree on big items. Used cars and new vehicles reprice
constantly, so the Atlanta Fed calls them flexible; but their *index* has serially
correlated monthly changes, so P3 calls them sticky. That single disagreement explains most
of the divergence: in June 2021 our sticky CPI hit 6.31% while our flexible was 3.12%,
because the used-car spike landed in our sticky basket and in the Atlanta Fed's flexible
one. In April–May 2026 the same thing runs the other way (AF flexible 5.46/6.76% vs ours
3.81/4.14%).

Over the 90 overlapping months the two sticky series correlate 0.59 (mean difference +0.05 pp,
MAE 1.33 pp) and the two flexible series correlate 0.76 (mean difference −0.27 pp, MAE
3.30 pp). Treat it as a sanity band, not a validation. Neither is "right" — they measure
different things and the labels happen to collide.

## Part 2 — technical appendix

### Universe and the monthly rate

Leaves are `cpi_weight WHERE is_leaf=1 AND matched=1`, per weight year (December relative
importance of year *Y* applies to the months of *Y+1*). Weight years present are
2017–2025; the union is 182 codes, 181 of which produce estimates.

Each item is assigned **one** monthly-rate definition, fixed for its whole history and
recorded in `p3_persist.source`:

| source | definition | items | weight |
|--------|-----------|-------|--------|
| `sa` | `mom_sa`, published SA month-over-month % change | 146 | 86.0% of CPI-U |
| `nsa12` | `100 × (ln P_t − ln P_{t−12}) / 12` | 36 | 10.2% |

An item uses `sa` if its SA series has at least 96 monthly changes; otherwise `nsa12`.

> **The `nsa12` filter manufactures persistence.** `(ln P_t − ln P_{t−12})/12` is the mean
> of the last twelve monthly log changes, i.e. a 12-term moving average. Applied to i.i.d.
> noise it produces a lag-1 autocorrelation of 11/12 = 0.917 analytically (half-life 8.0
> months); the pipeline re-derives it by simulation on 120-observation windows and gets
> rho = 0.896, half-life 6.3 months, the difference being the small-sample downward bias of
> OLS AR(1). It is logged every run as `summary.nsa12_null`.
>
> Consequences, all enforced in code: (a) every league table in the log and in the JSON is
> **within source group** — `stickiest`/`most_flexible` cover `sa` items only, and the
> `*_nsa12` lists are kept separately; (b) the rank normalisation that builds the sticky
> weights is computed within source group; (c) the pooled bucket table (`buckets.all`) is
> published but `buckets.by_source` is the one to use. 68.3% of `nsa12` weight lands in the
> ">12 months" bucket against 0.0% of `sa` weight — that gap is the artefact, not a finding.
>
> Robustness check: for `nsa12` items we also compute a half-life from month-of-year
> demeaned NSA MoM over the same window. Spearman between the two rankings is **0.609**
> over 31 items — the ordering survives, the level does not.

`universe = 'relative'` repeats everything on the item rate minus the headline (SA0) rate
put through the *same* filter (SA0 `mom_sa` for `sa` items, SA0's `nsa12` rate for `nsa12`
items), so the common component is differenced out.

### Estimates (rolling 120-month window ending at `ym`, re-estimated monthly from 2010-01)

* **rho** — OLS slope of `x_t` on `x_{t−1}` with a constant (equivalent to demeaned AR(1)).
* **halflife** — `ln(0.5)/ln(rho)` months; `0` if rho ≤ 0; capped at 60 (rho ≥ 1 → 60).
* **ar3sum** — sum of `b1..b3` from `x_t = a + b1 x_{t−1} + b2 x_{t−2} + b3 x_{t−3} + e`.
  Values above 1 mean the fitted process is non-stationary in that window; where `ar3sum`
  is far from `rho`, the AR(1) half-life is a poor summary of the item.
* **lp1 / lp3 / lp6 / lp12** — local projections: OLS slope of `x_{s+h}` on `x_s` with a
  constant, over the window, restricted to pairs with `s + h ≤ ym` so nothing uses data
  published after the window closes. `lp12` is the "how much is still there after a year"
  number.
* **lp12_se** — Newey–West (Bartlett kernel) HAC standard error of `lp12`, bandwidth
  `h + 1 = 13`, with an `n/(n−2)` small-sample correction. Overlapping horizons make LP
  residuals MA(h−1); the OLS SE would be badly understated. The HAC sum is built from a
  zero-padded score vector indexed by *window position*, so the October-2025 hole kills the
  cross-products that span it instead of silently pretending the series is contiguous.
* **pctile** — percentile of this month's half-life within the item's own history of
  half-lives up to and including this month (expanding, needs ≥ 12 prior windows, no
  look-ahead).
* **n_obs** — usable AR(1) pairs in the window.

An item enters a window only with ≥ 96 monthly rates (8 years) inside the 120 months. All
lags and leads are calendar-aligned on a complete monthly grid; October 2025 (no CPI,
shutdown) is a NaN row, never a row shift.

### Coverage gates

* **Months** (`p3_persist`) are dropped if items carrying < 90% of that weight year's leaf
  weight produced an estimate. Realised coverage 2010-01…2026-07: min 90.7%, median 97.6%,
  latest 98.6%. No month was dropped.
* **Index months** use the same `MIN_COV = 0.90` rule inside the chainer as P1/P2.

### Sticky / flexible construction

`dec_chained` / `Chainer` in `p3_persistence.py` is a **verbatim copy** of
`p1_salience.dec_chained` / `Chainer` (it is the comparison standard and
forbids importing across pipeline files). Only the weights change.

For weight year *Y*, the classification uses the raw-universe rho estimated on the window
ending **December of Y** — so the index for the months of *Y+1* never uses data from the
months it is applied to. Let `s_i` be the within-source rank percentile of rho,
`(rank − 0.5)/n ∈ (0,1)`:

| scheme | weight |
|--------|--------|
| `official_replica` | `w_i` (this pipeline's item subset; **P1's `p1_index.official_replica` is untouched**) |
| `sticky_cpi` | `w_i · s_i` |
| `flexible_cpi` | `w_i · (1 − s_i)` |
| `sticky_cpi_binary` / `flexible_cpi_binary` | hard split at the within-source weighted median of rho |
| `sticky_cpi_pooled` / `flexible_cpi_pooled` | `s_i = clip(rho_i, 0, 0.99)` pooled across sources — a deliberate sensitivity run showing what the `nsa12` artefact does if you do *not* normalise within source |

All schemes run on exactly the same item set, so they are comparable with each other and
with `official_replica`. Classification coverage runs 95.4% (2017) to 99.6% of leaf weight;
the sticky weight share of total weight is stable at 75–78%, the binary split at 50–55%.

**Validation.** `p3_index.official_replica` reproduces the published headline: last six
months −0.11, −0.05, −0.04, −0.00, −0.05, −0.06 pp (all inside the ±0.15 pp tolerance,
`validation.check_name = 'p3_official_replica_yoy'`). The Atlanta Fed rows
(`p3_sticky_vs_atlfed_yoy`, `p3_flexible_vs_atlfed_yoy`) use a ±2 pp *sanity band*, not a
tolerance — the two constructions are not supposed to agree.

### Common-shock caveat, quantified

Spearman correlation between raw and relative half-lives at 2026-07: **0.951** over 174
items, **0.909** over the 142 `sa` items. The *ordering* is robust; the *levels* are not.
Examples (`sa`, 2026-07):

| item | rho raw | half-life raw | rho relative | half-life relative |
|------|---------|---------------|--------------|--------------------|
| Owners' equivalent rent | 0.854 | 4.39 m | 0.493 | 0.98 m |
| Rent of primary residence | 0.848 | 4.20 m | 0.527 | 1.08 m |
| Airline fares | 0.605 | 1.38 m | 0.591 | 1.32 m |

Most of what looks like OER's own persistence is persistence it shares with the aggregate.
**But note the mechanical part of that**: OER is 25% of the headline, so subtracting the
headline subtracts a quarter of OER from itself. The relative universe is biased toward
finding *low* persistence for high-weight items, and the true answer for OER is somewhere
between 0.98 and 4.39 months. For small-weight items the relative measure is clean. Publish
both, never just one.

### Caveats (do not strip these from any front-end)

1. **Univariate persistence is not own persistence.** A slowly-fading common shock makes
   every item look sticky. Compare `universe='raw'` with `universe='relative'`, and read
   the bias note above before trusting the relative number for a large item.
2. **Rolling-window instability.** rho from 120 observations has a standard error of roughly
   0.09. A half-life of 4 months and one of 6 are usually not distinguishable. OER's own
   rolling rho ran 0.772 (2010) → 0.807 (2015) → 0.803 (2020) → 0.868 (2022-06) → 0.899
   (2024) → 0.854 (now) — real movement, but each point carries a wide band. `lp12_se` is
   the only uncertainty number published; treat the league table as indicative ordering.
3. **SA and NSA-derived rates are mixed.** See the `nsa12` box. Never rank an `sa` item
   against an `nsa12` item, and never quote the pooled bucket table without the by-source
   split next to it.
4. **AR(1) is a summary, not a model.** Where `ar3sum` differs materially from `rho`, the
   half-life is a poor description of that item's dynamics.
5. **No causal claim, and "sticky" here is statistical persistence — not menu-cost
   stickiness.** What P3 measures is the AR(1) persistence of the *published index's monthly
   inflation rate*: a time-series property of an aggregate series in a sample. It is **not** the
   micro "price stickiness" of Bils–Klenow / Nakamura–Steinsson, which is the *frequency with
   which individual firms actually change their posted prices* (the basis for the Atlanta Fed
   Sticky-Price CPI). The two can diverge sharply and do. The cleanest example is **used cars
   and trucks**: prices are auction-driven and reset constantly, so on the menu-cost /
   price-change-frequency definition they are a textbook *flexible*-price item and the Atlanta
   Fed classifies them as flexible — yet their published index has high lag-1 autocorrelation
   (driven by inventory and wholesale-auction cycles, not by infrequent repricing), so P3's
   AR(1) filter labels them **sticky** (used cars sit in the sticky basket at 3.5% of its
   weight, §Part 1). A high AR(1) is evidence the *index moves in serially correlated steps*,
   not that *firms hold prices fixed*. So this is not a statement about price-setting behaviour,
   about menu costs, or about what monetary policy will do — see "Reading the Atlanta Fed
   comparison" for the same point quantified. Tier 1 outputs must not imply otherwise.
6. **Classification churn.** The December re-classification can move an item between the
   sticky and flexible tilts from one year to the next; the index is chained through those
   Decembers, so a level comparison across many years mixes classifications. The
   `*_binary` schemes churn more than the continuous ones.

### Tables

```sql
p3_persist(ym, item_code, universe, rho, halflife, ar3sum,
           lp1, lp3, lp6, lp12, lp12_se, pctile, n_obs, source)   -- PK (ym, item_code, universe)
p3_index(scheme, ym, idx, yoy)                                     -- PK (scheme, ym)
```
66,980 rows over 199 months × 181 items × 2 universes; 7 schemes in `p3_index`, 2018-01…2026-07.
Both tables are fully rewritten on every run (idempotent).

### JSON

`$CPI_ROOT/out/p3_persistence.json` (~200 KB). Schema is documented in the file's own
`schema` block and in the module docstring. Top-level keys: `treemap` (latest-month cross
section, one object per leaf item, both universes — the treemap payload), `series` (all 7
schemes, `ym`/`idx`/`yoy`), `classification` (per-December diagnostics), `atlanta_fed`
(their series, a 12-month log-difference comparison for the last 12 months, and correlations
over the whole overlap), `summary` (buckets, Spearman raw-vs-relative, league tables,
scheme composition, coverage path, `nsa12_null`, `nsa12_robustness`), `notes`.

Dashboard note: the `treemap` array carries `source` on every item. Any visual that sorts
or colours by half-life must either facet by `source` or drop the `nsa12` items, otherwise
it will show a chart of the filter artefact.

### Cron

P3 depends on P0 only (it reads `cpi_item_month`, `cpi_weight`, `cu_item`). It must run in
the **"P0 loaded new data"** branch of `pipelines/run_monthly.sh`, immediately after the P2
line (P3 does not read anything P1 or P2 write, so the order between them is free):

```sh
echo "== $(date -Is) p3" >> $LOG
# exit 2 = the P3 headline replica drifted outside ±0.15 pp; tables/JSON are still written
./p3_persistence.py >> $LOG 2>&1 || echo "WARNING: P3 validation flagged — see above" >> $LOG
```

and, in the February weight-refresh branch, chained after the P2 re-run so the new December
weights and the new December classification are picked up:

```sh
./p3_persistence.py >> $LOG 2>&1 || echo "WARNING: P3 validation flagged after weight refresh" >> $LOG
```

Runtime ~35 s (19 s of it the 66,980 rolling regressions), plus four small FRED downloads.
`--no-fred` skips the Atlanta Fed cross-check if FRED is unreachable; the pipeline already
degrades gracefully if the fetch fails.
