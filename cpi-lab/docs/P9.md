# P9 — Metro CPI vs local wages and rents

`pipelines/p9_metro.py` → tables `p9_*`, output `$CPI_ROOT/out/p9_metro.json`

---

## Part 1 — What this is, in plain language

National inflation is an average, and the average is not what anybody pays. The CPI is
actually priced in 23 large metro areas that are big enough to get their own published index,
and those 23 numbers spread out a long way. In July 2026 the metro all-items 12‑month changes
ranged from **+1.9 % (Tampa)** to **+5.6 % (Urban Hawaii)** — a 3.7‑point spread around a
population‑weighted mean of 3.5 %.

This pipeline takes those 23 local inflation rates and puts them next to two things people
actually feel locally:

**1. Local pay.** BLS's QCEW counts the wages on essentially every payroll in the country from
unemployment‑insurance filings, by metro, every quarter. Subtracting local inflation from local
pay growth gives a rough "did pay keep up here?" number. In **2025Q4** it kept up almost
everywhere, but by wildly different margins:

| Fastest real pay growth (2025Q4) | | Slowest |  |
|---|---|---|---|
| San Francisco | **+3.2 pp** | Riverside | **−2.8 pp** |
| Miami | +3.1 pp | Seattle | −2.4 pp |
| Houston | +2.9 pp | San Diego | −1.2 pp |

Read those as percentage points of wage growth *after* local inflation. Riverside's pay grew
1.7 % while its prices rose 4.5 %; San Francisco's pay grew 6.3 % while its prices rose 3.0 %.
This is not a statement about any individual's paycheck — see the caveats.

**2. Rent.** The CPI measures rent across *all* leases, most of which were signed years ago,
so it turns slowly. Zillow's ZORI measures what landlords are asking on *new* leases, so it
turns fast. The famous consequence is that the CPI's rent number is a lagged echo of the market:
across these 23 metros the CPI rent series correlates best with market asking rents from
**about a year earlier** (median best lag **12 months**, metro range 7–18).

Comparing the CPI's current rent inflation with market rent growth a year earlier tells you
whether the CPI still has catching‑up to do. In July 2026 the biggest remaining gaps were
**Miami (+2.3 pp)** and **Denver (+2.1 pp)** — CPI rent still running above where the market
was a year ago — and the biggest gaps the other way were **San Francisco (−1.5 pp)** and
**Houston (−1.3 pp)**, where the CPI has already overshot the market signal.

**3. How spread out are metros?** In the quiet years before COVID the population‑weighted
standard deviation across metros sat around **0.77 pp**. It peaked at **1.86 pp in August 2022**
(when Phoenix was at 13.0 % and San Francisco at 5.7 % *in the same month*). As of July 2026 it
is back to **0.81 pp** — essentially the pre‑COVID normal. The great metro divergence of
2021‑23 has closed.

### The catch you have to know about

**Only 3 of the 23 metros publish an all‑items index every month** (New York, Chicago, Los
Angeles). The other 20 publish every *other* month, split into two staggered groups. So the
"metro inflation this month" cross‑section alternates between 12 cities and 14 cities, and the
average can move a quarter of a point purely because the cast changed. Every table here reports
only the metros that actually published that month, and flags the months where the swap is
visibly moving the average. Nothing is interpolated, ever.

## Part 2 — Technical appendix

### 2.1 The metro universe

BLS prices 23 "self‑representing" areas — metros large enough to be their own primary sampling
unit rather than a draw from a region/size‑class stratum. The CPI `area_code` in `cu.area` is
the PSU code (`S12A` etc.), which is what this pipeline keys on.

| schedule | metros |
|---|---|
| monthly (3) | New York `S12A`, Chicago `S23A`, Los Angeles `S49A` |
| odd months (9) | Boston `S11A`, Minneapolis `S24A`, Washington `S35A`, Tampa `S35D`, Dallas `S37A`, Denver `S48B`, Riverside `S49C`, San Diego `S49E`, Urban Hawaii `S49F` |
| even months (11) | Philadelphia `S12B`, **Detroit `S23B`**, St. Louis `S24B`, Miami `S35B`, Atlanta `S35C`, Baltimore `S35E`, Houston `S37B`, Phoenix `S48A`, San Francisco `S49B`, Seattle `S49D`, Urban Alaska `S49G` |

Three of the 23 (Tampa, Phoenix, Riverside) only begin in **2017‑11/2017‑12**, when BLS added
them in the 2018 geographic revision. Phoenix is on a `DECEMBER 2001=100` base and Riverside on
`DECEMBER 2017=100`; Tampa is `1987=100`; the other 20 are `1982‑84=100`. Index *levels* are
therefore never compared across metros — only 12‑month changes, which are base‑invariant.

The areas suspended in 2025 (Lincoln, Provo, Buffalo) were **non‑self‑representing** sample
areas; they never had a published metro index and correctly do not appear here.

> **Correction to the brief.** The task brief listed Detroit `S23B` on the odd (Jan/Mar/…)
> schedule. The flat file says otherwise: `CUURS23BSA0` carries values in Feb/Apr/Jun/… and is
> **even**. That makes the split 9 odd / 11 even / 3 monthly, not 10/10/3. Because published
> reference lists get this wrong, `derive_schedule()` **derives** the schedule from the last
> five years of published `SA0` months rather than hard‑coding it, and logs the result on every
> run. The schedule label is descriptive only — no downstream calculation depends on it (see
> §2.4), so a future BLS reshuffle cannot silently corrupt a number.

### 2.2 Bimonthly applies to all‑items and food — **not** to shelter or energy

This is the single most useful thing found while building P9, and it is not obvious from the
BLS documentation. Verified directly in `cu.data.0.Current` (rows since 2024‑01, all 23 metros):

| item | rows | distinct months |
|---|---|---|
| `SA0` all items | 388 | 30 |
| `SAF` food | 388 | 30 |
| `SAH1` shelter | 690 | 30 |
| `SEHA` rent of primary residence | 690 | 30 |
| `SEHC01` owners' equivalent rent | 690 | 30 |
| `SA0E` energy | 688 | 30 |

690 = 23 metros × 30 months, i.e. **complete monthly coverage for every metro**. The housing
survey and fuel/utility pricing run monthly everywhere; the general commodity and service
pricing that feeds all‑items does not. Practical consequence:

* `p9_shelter` and the lag correlations are **monthly for all 23 metros** (n = 91–118 monthly
  observations per metro in the lag profile, not 45–60).
* `p9_real` and `p9_disp` are built on `SA0` and therefore carry the full bimonthly stagger.

### 2.3 October 2025

There is no CPI for 2025‑10 for anyone. BLS ships the row anyway with `value = '-'` and
`footnote_codes = 'X'`; numeric coercion turns it into NULL and the loader drops it. Checked
for `CUUR0000SA0`, `CUURS12ASA0`, `CUURS12BSA0`, `CUURS12BSEHA` — all `'-' / 'X'`.

For odd‑schedule metros October was not their month, so nothing is lost. For **even‑schedule
metros** it removes a scheduled observation and leaves a four‑month hole: Philadelphia's
all‑items index goes 2025‑08 → 2025‑12. Their **2026‑10 YoY will be uncomputable** and will
appear as NULL; that is correct, not a bug. Every lag in this pipeline is a calendar‑aligned
merge on a shifted `PeriodIndex` (`lag_merge()`), never a row offset — a `shift(12)` would have
silently paired 2026‑12 with 2025‑08.

### 2.4 The quarter‑matching rule

A quarterly wage growth rate has to be matched to a monthly inflation rate. The rule:

> **The CPI reading for a quarter is the last month *inside that quarter* for which the metro
> actually published an all‑items index.**

|  | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| monthly metros | Mar | Jun | Sep | Dec |
| odd metros | Mar | May | Sep | Nov |
| even metros | Feb | Jun | Aug | Dec |

Why this rule:

* **Always inside the quarter.** Reaching forward to the next quarter's month (e.g. using April
  for Q1) would leak information the quarter did not have.
* **Always the freshest reading available**, so the series is as current as it can be.
* **Stable over time**, so a metro's quarterly real‑wage series is not spliced together from
  different months of the quarter in different years.
* **Data‑driven, not schedule‑driven.** It is implemented as "the max published month within
  the quarter", so it self‑corrects when the schedule changed historically (a handful of
  2016‑17 quarters resolve to a different month than the table above, because BLS reassigned
  bimonthly slots in the 2018 revision) and when a month is missing.

The month actually used is stored per row in `p9_real.cpi_month_used`, so nothing is hidden.
The Oct‑2025 hole is visible there: every even‑schedule metro's 2025Q4 row reads `2025-12`
(the rule falls through October automatically), every odd metro reads `2025-11`.

`real_yoy = wage_yoy − cpi_yoy`, in percentage points, as specified. The exact ratio form
`((1+w)/(1+c) − 1)` differs by under 0.1 pp at these rates.

### 2.5 The staggering artifact, and how it is flagged

In an odd month the cross‑section is 12 metros; in an even month it is 14 *different* metros.
The population‑weighted mean therefore jumps between adjacent months for reasons unrelated to
prices. `p9_disp` measures exactly that:

```
comp_delta = mean[t] − mean(mean[t−1], mean[t], mean[t+1])
comp_flag  = 1 when |comp_delta| > 0.3 pp
```

Because `t−1` and `t+1` carry the complementary panel, the three‑month centred average is close
to composition‑neutral, and the residual is the composition effect. `comp_delta` is NULL (and
the flag NULL) when either neighbour is missing — including the month either side of the
Oct‑2025 hole and the latest month.

**15 of 212 months are flagged.** They cluster exactly where you would predict: mid‑2022 to
mid‑2023, when metro inflation rates were furthest apart and so the panel swap mattered most.
The pattern alternates in sign — 2022‑06 `+0.73`, 2022‑07 `−0.59`, 2022‑08 `+0.49`, 2022‑09
`−0.35`, 2022‑10 `+0.50`, 2022‑11 `−0.40` — which is the signature of a composition artifact,
not of inflation. In that stretch the even panel (Phoenix, Miami, Atlanta) was running roughly
a point hotter than the odd panel (New York, Boston, Washington).

Other rules for `p9_disp`:
* Only metros with a published value that month enter the cross‑section; **n ≥ 8 required**.
* Weights are 2020 CBSA population, constant over time.
* `sd` is the population‑weighted standard deviation, `sqrt(Σwᵢ(xᵢ−x̄)²/Σwᵢ)`.
* Series starts at `DISP_FROM = 1998-01` even if `--history` loaded the 1914+ data, because BLS
  redefined the metro pricing areas in 1998 (and again in 2018) — extending it further would
  silently change geography under fixed area codes.

### 2.6 QCEW wages

**Endpoint.** `https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/{year}/{qtr}/industry/10.csv` — one 3.7 MB
file per quarter covering every area. The per‑area slice documented on the BLS
[CSV data slices](https://www.bls.gov/cew/additional-resources/open-data/csv-data-slices.htm)
page (`.../area/C3562.csv`) returns identical numbers but needs 23× the requests. MSA rows have
`area_fips` = `'C'` + the first four digits of the CBSA code (35620 → `C3562`); `agglvl_code`
40 = MSA total, 41 = MSA by ownership. We keep `own_code` 0 (total covered) and 5 (private),
`industry_code` 10 (all industries).

**Caching.** The cache in `raw/qcew/ind10_YYYYqN.csv` holds only the ~46 filtered rows, not the
3.7 MB source — the slice covers every county, state and CSA in the country and we need 23
metros of it. Existing quarters are skipped; the two most recent cached quarters are re‑fetched
each run because QCEW revises backwards. `--force` re‑fetches everything.

**Lag.** Longer than the "~5 months" in the brief. Checked 2026‑08‑24: **2025Q4 is the latest
published quarter and 2026Q1 returns HTTP 404**. QCEW posts a quarter roughly 5–6 months after
it ends, so on 2026‑08‑24 that means Q4‑2025 (published early June 2026), with Q1‑2026 due in
September. Effective lag from *today* to the latest data point is therefore **7–8 months**, and
`p9_real` will always trail `p9_cpi` by two to three quarters.

**Private‑ownership suppression — the real gotcha.** MSA‑level `own_code 5` (private) carries
`disclosure_code '-'` and zeros **from 2025Q3 onward, for every MSA**. Verified quarter by
quarter for New York: `own 5` is present and non‑zero for 2019Q1…2025Q2 and suppressed for
2025Q3 and 2025Q4, while `own 0` (total covered) is published throughout.

The handling:

* `p9_wage` stores **both** ownership bases, with `disclosed = 0` and a NULL wage for suppressed
  cells (BLS writes suppressed cells as literal zeros — storing those as zeros would produce
  a −100 % wage change).
* YoY is computed **within** an ownership basis. A YoY is never taken across bases.
* `p9_real` prefers the private YoY and falls back to total covered, recording which in
  `wage_own`. Currently **757 of 817 rows use private; 60 rows (2025Q3–Q4, all 23 metros in Q4)
  use total covered.**
* How much does the basis matter? Where both exist, private and total‑covered wage YoY differ
  by **0.34 pp on average**. That is not nothing: the 2025Q4 cross‑metro *ranking* is on a
  slightly different footing than earlier quarters, and any chart should mark it. The last
  fully private‑basis quarter is **2025Q2**, whose ranking is led by Urban Alaska (+3.8 pp),
  Phoenix (+3.5) and San Francisco (+3.4), with Seattle (−2.1), Los Angeles (−1.5) and San
  Diego (−1.4) at the bottom — a broadly similar picture, which is reassuring.

**What the wage number is.** `avg_wkly_wage` = total quarterly wages ÷ (13 × average monthly
employment) over all covered jobs. It is per *job*, not per person, it includes bonuses (hence
the enormous Q1/Q4 seasonality — always compare year over year), and it moves with employment
composition: a metro that sheds low‑wage jobs shows wage growth without anyone getting a raise.

### 2.7 ZORI

`Metro_zori_uc_sfrcondomfr_sm_month.csv` from `files.zillowstatic.com/research/public_csvs/zori/`
— smoothed, all home types (SFR + condo + multifamily), **not** seasonally adjusted (the `_sa_`
variant exists and is listed as a fallback candidate). 752 metro rows, monthly from **2015‑01**
to **2026‑07**, one column per month.

**Fail‑soft, because Zillow reshuffles these files roughly annually.** `load_zori()`:

1. walks `ZORI_URLS` in preference order;
2. accepts the first response that *parses into the expected shape* — a `RegionName` column plus
   ≥ 60 `YYYY-MM-DD` columns — rather than trusting HTTP 200;
3. writes the accepted file to `raw/zori/Metro_zori_last_good.csv` and the winning URL plus a
   timestamp to `raw/zori/last_good.url`;
4. if **every** candidate fails, loads the last good copy, logs a loud warning naming the file's
   age and pointing at <https://www.zillow.com/research/data/>, and carries on;
5. only if there is no cache either does it skip the step, leaving `p9_zori`/`p9_shelter`
   untouched rather than emptying them.

The resolved URL is logged every run and echoed in the JSON at `coverage.zori_source`, so a
silent switch to a fallback file is visible.

**Crosswalk.** ZORI keys on its own `RegionName` strings (`"New York, NY"`, `"Urban Honolulu,
HI"`), not CBSA codes, so the mapping is by name in `CROSSWALK`. **23 of 23 matched**, all with
the full 139‑month history — comfortably over the ≥ 20 requirement.

**Concept mismatch, stated plainly.** CPI rent is the average rent paid across the whole stock
of occupied units, most under leases signed months or years ago, and it includes lease renewals
at below‑market rates. ZORI is a repeat‑rent index of *asking* rents on units newly listed. They
measure different things; the lag between them is a mechanical consequence of lease turnover,
not a forecast relationship, and neither one is "right".

### 2.8 The lag profile

For each metro, `corr(cpi_rent_yoy[t], zori_yoy[t − L])` for L = 0…18, requiring ≥ 24 overlapping
months. Stored in full in `p9_lag`; the argmax per metro is the "best lag".

* Median best lag **12 months**; at a fixed 12‑month lag the mean correlation across metros is
  **0.735**.
* Fast: Urban Alaska 7 (r = 0.89), Detroit and Minneapolis 8.
* Slow: St. Louis and Urban Hawaii 18, Riverside 17, Philadelphia 15.
* **Urban Hawaii is not usable.** Its best correlation at *any* lag is **0.07** — no
  relationship at all. This is the clearest symptom of the geography mismatch in §2.9: CPI
  "Urban Hawaii" is priced statewide while ZORI's `Urban Honolulu, HI` is Oahu only. Treat
  Hawaii's shelter gap as unreliable; the JSON marks it with `approx_geo: true`.
* Two metros (Detroit r = 0.55, Los Angeles r = 0.58) have visibly weaker relationships than the
  rest even at their best lag.

`p9_shelter.zori_yoy_l12` is fixed at 12 months for the headline gap so the metros are
comparable to each other; the per‑metro best lag is reported separately rather than being used
to build a metro‑specific gap that no two metros share a definition for.

### 2.9 Crosswalk and population

`CROSSWALK` in the source has one row per metro with a comment, mapping CPI `area_code` →
CBSA → QCEW `area_fips` → ZORI `RegionName` → 2020 population. Names differ across the three
agencies because the CPI area title is frozen at the vintage BLS defined the pricing area while
QCEW and Census use the current OMB title (Phoenix‑Mesa‑**Scottsdale** vs ‑**Chandler**,
Houston‑**The Woodlands‑Sugar Land** vs ‑**Pasadena‑The Woodlands**, San Francisco‑Oakland‑
**Hayward** vs ‑**Fremont**, Denver‑Aurora‑**Lakewood** vs ‑**Centennial**, Miami‑…‑**West Palm
Beach** vs ‑**Pompano Beach**, Atlanta‑…‑**Roswell** vs ‑**Alpharetta**, San Diego‑**Carlsbad**
vs ‑**Chula Vista‑Carlsbad**). The **geography is the same CBSA in all of them** — only the
title moved. Two exceptions, both flagged `approx_geo` in `p9_area` and in the JSON:

* **`S49F` Urban Hawaii → CBSA 46520 Urban Honolulu.** The CPI prices urban Hawaii statewide;
  Honolulu MSA is Oahu only. See the lag result in §2.8 — this join is weak.
* **`S49G` Urban Alaska → CBSA 11260 Anchorage.** The CPI covers urban Alaska (Anchorage plus
  Fairbanks); Anchorage MSA is the dominant but not the only piece.

Populations are hard‑coded April‑1‑2020 CBSA counts (`ESTIMATESBASE2020`) and cross‑checked at
run time against
`https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest/datasets/2020-2022/metro/totals/cbsa-est2022.csv`
— **23/23 within 2 %**. The check is fail‑soft: if Census moves the file, the run logs a warning
and uses the hard‑coded values. (The Census *API* now requires a key and returns a "Missing Key"
HTML page with HTTP 200 — do not use it here.) Population is used only as a dispersion weight,
so relative magnitudes are what matter.

Six metros are spot‑checked rather than three, deliberately covering both a monthly metro and
both bimonthly schedules. The releases print to 0.1 pp, so the tolerance is 0.06 pp. The parser
keys on the sentence *"…all items CPI‑U <verb> X.X percent for the 12 months ending in
<Month>"*, which is present in every regional release, with the subtitle *"up X.X percent over
the year"* as a fallback and the month/year taken from the release title. All of it is
fail‑soft: an unreachable or reworded release is logged as `ok: null`, not as a failure.

Results are written to the shared `validation` table with `check_name` prefixed `p9_`, and
`main()` exits **2** if a hard check fails (tables and JSON are still written).

### 2.12 What this does not say

* **No causal claim anywhere.** Real wage growth here is the arithmetic difference of two
  independently measured growth rates for the same geography. It is not any individual's change
  in purchasing power: QCEW wages are per covered job and move with employment composition, the
  CPI basket is the average urban consumer's and not the average worker's, and the two
  populations are not the same people.
* **Simpson's paradox in `avg_wkly_wage` — it is wage-per-*job*, not per-*person*.**
  `avg_wkly_wage = total_wages / (13 × employment)`, so it is a ratio of aggregates and moves
  with the *composition* of who is employed, not only with pay rates. A metro that adds
  high-wage jobs (a tech-hiring wave) or sheds low-wage ones will show rising average weekly
  wages **without any individual getting a raise** — and the reverse in a downturn that cuts
  high earners first. So a metro's `real_yoy` can be positive while typical workers' real pay
  is flat or falling (or vice versa). This is the classic Simpson's-paradox trap of comparing
  group means across a shifting mix; the number is a payroll-composition indicator, not a
  paycheck.
* **ZORI selection bias.** Zillow's ZORI is built from listings that are disproportionately
  **professionally managed / large-operator units** and from what landlords *ask* on newly
  listed units — not a representative draw of the occupied stock the CPI prices. The universe
  skews to the professionally-managed, higher-turnover end of the market, so ZORI can miss
  small-landlord and long-tenure segments entirely. In some metros the two barely relate at
  all: **Urban Hawaii's CPI rent has near-zero correlation with ZORI at every lag (best r =
  0.07, §2.8)** — partly the statewide-vs-Oahu geography mismatch, but the selection difference
  compounds it. Treat the shelter/ZORI gap as a comparison of two differently-selected rent
  concepts, and do not read a small gap as a signal.
* **QCEW private-wage suppression — a real series break at 2025Q3 (verified read-only against
  the live DB).** BLS suppresses MSA-level private employment (`own_code 5`) from **2025Q3
  onward**: confirmed in `p9_wage` that `own 5` is fully disclosed through 2025Q2 (22–23 of 23
  metros) and **fully suppressed (0 of 23) in 2025Q3 and 2025Q4**, while total-covered
  (`own 0`) is published throughout. `p9_real` therefore splices the total-covered basis onto a
  previously private-basis series at 2025Q3 (`wage_own` records which basis each row used).
  Where both bases exist, private and total-covered wage YoY differ by **0.34 pp on average**
  (measured 0.3366 pp over 860 metro-quarter pairs on the live data) — so the 2025Q4
  cross-metro ranking sits on a slightly different footing than earlier quarters and any chart
  crossing 2025Q3 should mark the basis change. The last fully private-basis quarter is
  **2025Q2**.
* The shelter/ZORI gap is a **description of two different rent concepts moving at different
  speeds**, not evidence that market rents drive the CPI.
* Metro CPI is **NSA only** — there is no seasonally adjusted metro CPI at all (zero `CUSR`
  metro series in `cu.series`). Month‑to‑month changes are not comparable to the seasonally
  adjusted national headline; only 12‑month changes are used here.
* Metro indexes are built on smaller samples than the national index and have materially larger
  sampling error. A 0.3 pp difference between two metros in one month is not a finding.
* Dispersion before 2018 covers 20 metros, not 23, and uses the pre‑2018 area definitions.

### 2.13 Operations

```
./p9_metro.py                     normal run (16 s measured, once QCEW/ZORI are cached)
./p9_metro.py --history           also load cu.data.7..10 (metro CPI back to 1914)
./p9_metro.py --force             re-download everything, rebuild
./p9_metro.py --no-qcew           skip the wage step
./p9_metro.py --no-zori           skip the Zillow step
./p9_metro.py --qcew-from 2010    extend the QCEW backfill
```

First run cost: 44 QCEW quarters (~3.7 MB each, ~4 min with the built-in 0.4 s courtesy delay)
plus a 1 MB ZORI file. Steady state is 2 QCEW quarters + ZORI + the cached CPI file.

**Cron placement (`run_monthly.sh` — not edited here, per the round‑3 rule).** P9 has two
independent clocks and should run **unconditionally**, not only on new CPI data:

* metro CPI changes only when P0 sees new data, but
* QCEW posts a new quarter in March/June/September/December on its own schedule, and ZORI
  refreshes monthly — both entirely independent of the CPI release calendar.

Running it unconditionally costs ~16 s on a no‑op day and keeps `p9_real` and `p9_shelter` from
sitting a month stale. Add it next to the other unconditional steps, after P0 (it reads
`cpi_item_month` for the P0 cross‑check):

```bash
# P9 (metro CPI vs local wages/rents): metro CPI only moves with new CPI data, but QCEW posts
# a quarter in Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec and ZORI refreshes monthly, both off the CPI calendar -> always.
# Needs cpi_item_month (P0) for its US-city-average cross-check.
```
