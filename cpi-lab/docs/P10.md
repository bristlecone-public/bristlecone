# P10 — which item prices move before which

`pipelines/p10_network.py` → tables `p10_edges`, `p10_nodes`, `p10_lagprofile`, `p10_meta`,
file `out/p10_network.json`

> **lead-lag correlation, not pass-through, not causal.**
> An arrow from *i* to *j* means that when *i*'s seasonally adjusted monthly rate moved,
> *j*'s rate tended to move a few months later. It does **not** mean *i*'s prices feed into
> *j*'s costs. Two items can lead and lag each other because they share a driver, because
> BLS prices them on staggered schedules, because one is smoothed and the other is not, or
> by chance. Everything below exists to stop the picture being a swamp of accidents — none
> of it turns a correlation into a cause.

---

## Part 1 — in plain language

### What is being drawn

74 mid-level CPI items — rent, gasoline, food away from home, airline fares, appliances,
college tuition and so on, covering **89% of what households spend** — are treated as 74
monthly series of price changes since 2000. For every one of the **5,402 ordered pairs**
("does A move before B?" is a different question from "does B move before A?") we ask
whether A's move this month lines up with B's move one to twelve months later, and how
reliably.

Only the relationships that survive four separate filters get drawn as an arrow. Out of
5,402 candidate pairs, **70 survive** in the main view.

### The two views

| view | what it shows |
|---|---|
| **`raw`** | The items as published. This view is *dominated by energy*: fuel oil, gasoline and energy services move first and everything else follows, because energy is what moves the overall inflation number and the overall number is inside every series. 88 arrows. |
| **`common_removed`** | Each item with the general inflation tide subtracted, leaving only its own idiosyncratic movement. **This is the view to read.** 70 arrows. |

The difference between the two is itself the most interesting result. Almost every
energy→transport link — gasoline leading airline fares by a month, fuel oil leading
airline fares — is **strong in `raw` and gone in `common_removed`**. That is not a bug: it
says those items do not have a private relationship with each other, they are both simply
riding the same energy-driven inflation tide. Read as economics: airline fares track
energy because energy moves everything, not because there is an airline-specific fuel
channel visible at monthly frequency in these indexes.

Three things stand out.

**1. The grocery-to-restaurant lag is real and it is three months.** Every food-at-home
group — cereals and bakery, other food at home, nonalcoholic beverages, meat/poultry/fish/
eggs — leads **food away from home** by 2–3 months with ρ between +0.30 and +0.43, in 3 or
4 of the 4 windows, and it survives removing the common inflation factor. This is the
clearest genuinely item-specific lead in the whole CPI.

**2. Rent and owners' equivalent rent are effectively the same series offset by a month**
(ρ = 0.80 in *both* directions). That is expected: BLS builds OER from the same rent sample
using the same six-month rotating panel, so this edge is a methodology link, not a market
one. It is a useful positive control — if the method could not find this, nothing else it
found would be trustworthy.

**3. Nothing leads by a long horizon.** Almost every surviving arrow is 1–5 months. The
map does not support "watch X to know where inflation goes next year".

### What emphatically does *not* show up

- **Shelter does not lead the rest of the basket.** Rent has the highest "upstreamness"
  score of any node, but its outgoing arrows go to food away from home, tools and used
  cars, with ρ ≈ 0.25–0.5 — not to the basket at large.
- **Energy leads nothing item-specific.** After the common factor is removed, gasoline has
  two weak outgoing arrows and fuel oil has none.
- **Medical care, apparel and recreation are almost entirely disconnected.** 15 apparel
  nodes produce one arrow between them. Their monthly changes are noise plus the common
  tide, and nothing else.

The honest summary of the main view: **once you take out the general inflation tide, CPI
items barely lead each other at all.** 70 arrows out of 5,402 possible, most of them weak,
and the strongest ones are a food chain and a BLS methodology link. That is a finding, not
a failure — it is a direct argument against the folk model where a shock enters at one item
and cascades predictably through the basket.

### How to read a number

- **ρ (rho)** runs from −1 to +1 and is how tightly the two move together at that lag.
  ρ = 0.43 is a visible tendency, not a rule: it means roughly 18% of the follower's
  month-to-month wiggle lines up with the leader's three months earlier.
- **Lag** is the *best* lag, not the only one. Neighbouring lags usually look similar; the
  `p10_lagprofile` table holds all twelve so you can see the shape.
- **q** is the false-discovery-rate-adjusted p-value of a Granger test. A *low* q means the
  leader tells you something about the follower **that the follower's own history did not
  already tell you**. A high q with a high ρ — like rent → OER, ρ = 0.80, q = 0.78 — means
  "these two move together with a one-month offset, but neither predicts the other beyond
  its own past". Both numbers are shown because they answer different questions.

## Part 2 — technical appendix

### Universe

Nodes are non-overlapping published items at `cu_item` display levels 2–3 under the eight
major groups (SAF, SAH, SAA, SAT, SAM, SAR, SAE, SAG), requiring a seasonally adjusted
index with `mom_sa` present for ≥ 80% of months since 2000-01 and data through 2026-01 or
later. Three selection rules are implemented (`--universe`); **`hybrid` is the default**:

| rule | construction | nodes | basket covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| `coarse` | every usable level-2 item; descend to level 3 only where the level-2 item has no usable SA series | 53 | 90.9% |
| **`hybrid`** | `coarse`, then split a node into its usable level-3 children **only if those children hold ≥ 90% of the parent's relative importance** | **74** | **88.9%** |
| `fine` | finest available among levels 2–3 | 84 | 80.0% |

`hybrid` is 74 nodes (38 at level 2, 36 at level 3) rather than the ~100 a finer cut would
give, because the split rule refuses to break a parent apart when the published children do
not reproduce it. `fine` reaches 84 nodes only by replacing, for example, "Furniture and
bedding" (0.88% of the basket) with its one published child "Other furniture", silently
dropping the rest — and 9 of its 84 nodes have no relative importance at all. Being
*exhaustive* matters more for reading a network than node count, and expenditure-class
aggregates have a better signal-to-noise ratio than leaves, so a coarser graph is a less
spurious graph.

By construction **no node is an ancestor of another**, so no edge can be a mechanical
aggregation identity. Group coverage is uneven and this is a real limitation: housing 15
nodes / 42.3% of the basket, apparel 15 nodes / 2.2%, "other goods and services" only 2
nodes / 1.1% (cigarettes and personal care products have no mid-level SA series reaching
back far enough).

### Panel

`cpi_item_month.mom_sa`, 2000-01 … 2026-07, reindexed to a **complete monthly calendar** so
a *k*-row shift is exactly a *k*-calendar-month lag — October 2025 has no CPI, and row-offset
lags would silently splice September to November. A month is blanked when items covering
< 60% of the universe's weight have a value, which drops **2025-10** (no CPI) and
**2025-11** (MoM undefined), the same gate P2 uses. 319 calendar months, 23,255 of 23,606
cells present.

### Variants

`raw` — each series demeaned over the estimation window.

`common_removed` — each series regressed on the headline SA MoM (`SA0`) over the window,
residual retained. The headline is taken **leave-one-out**:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*h*<sub>−*i*,*t*</sub> = (*h<sub>t</sub>* − *w<sub>it</sub>* *x<sub>it</sub>* / 100) / (1 − *w<sub>it</sub>* / 100)

with *w<sub>it</sub>* the item's relative importance that month (`cpi_item_month.weight`;
vintages start Dec-2017 and are back-filled before that).

**This is the single most consequential choice in P10 and it is a deliberate departure
from "regress on headline SA MoM".** Gasoline is 2.9% of the basket but its SA monthly rate
has standard deviation 5.70 against the headline's 0.30 — 19× — so most of
cov(gasoline, headline) is gasoline regressing on *itself* through its own weight. Its
coefficient on the plain headline is **+16.4**, which makes its residual approximately
−16.4 × headline. Every persistent service item is positively correlated with the *lagged*
headline, so the plain-headline version hands gasoline a **flat** ρ ≈ −0.4 … −0.5 arrow into
rent, food away from home and vehicle maintenance **at every lag from 1 to 12** — the
signature of a level artifact, not of a lead-lag relation. Measured effect of the fix:

| | plain headline | leave-one-out |
|---|---|---|
| ordered pairs with ρ<sub>best</sub> > 0.25 | 181 | 170 |
| energy items (gasoline / fuel oil / energy services) as source | 12 | **3** |
| gasoline → rent | −0.51 @ lag 4 | **−0.24** @ lag 4 |
| gasoline → vehicle maintenance | −0.36 @ lag 4 | **−0.18** @ lag 4 |
| rent → OER | +0.796 | +0.797 |
| other food at home → food away | +0.416 | +0.427 |
| used cars → new vehicles | +0.291 | +0.315 |

Every artifact shrinks; every genuine edge is preserved or slightly stronger. `--no-loo`
reproduces the plain-headline version. `--factor-lags=K` additionally projects off
*h*<sub>−*i*,*t*−1…*t*−*K*</sub>, which removes the *remaining* energy artifacts entirely
(K = 12 leaves zero energy sources) but attenuates real edges by ~30% at K = 6 and ~40% at
K = 12 — at K = 12 the food chain falls to ρ = 0.21–0.25 and only 19 edges survive. Default
K = 0: leave-one-out alone does the job.

Three weak negative gasoline/fuel-oil edges do survive into the main view
(rent → gasoline −0.26, gasoline → OER −0.28, rent → fuel oil −0.28). They are residual
artifact. Every stable edge in the JSON therefore carries **`rho_other`**, the same pair at
the same lag measured in the other variant, so an edge created by the factor step is
visible as one.

### Statistics

Per ordered pair *i* → *j*, per variant, per window:

1. **Cross-correlation** ρ*<sub>k</sub>* = corr(*x<sub>i,t−k</sub>*, *x<sub>j,t</sub>*) for
   *k* = 1…12, pairwise-complete. Computed in closed form as six matrix products per lag
   (*n*, Σ*x<sub>i</sub>*, Σ*x<sub>j</sub>*, Σ*x<sub>i</sub>²*, Σ*x<sub>j</sub>²*,
   Σ*x<sub>i</sub>x<sub>j</sub>* over the mutually-observed months, all as masked
   *X*ᵀ*X* products), so all 5,402 pairs × 12 lags cost twelve passes, not 64,824 loops.
   **best lag** = argmax*<sub>k</sub>* |ρ*<sub>k</sub>*|.
2. **Granger-style F test.** Regress *x<sub>j,t</sub>* on a constant, its own lags 1…6 and
   *x<sub>i</sub>* lags 1…6; Wald-test the six *x<sub>i</sub>* coefficients jointly with a
   **Newey–West HAC** covariance (Bartlett kernel, *L* = ⌊4(*n*/100)<sup>2/9</sup>⌋ = 5,
   small-sample factor *n*/(*n*−*k*)). *F* ~ *F*(6, *n*−13). Implemented as batched numpy —
   one (74, T, 13) design tensor per source item, `pinv`-solved — because 5,402 pairs ×
   5 windows × 2 variants is 52,560 regressions. Pairs with < 60 usable observations are
   not tested.
3. **Multiple comparisons.** Benjamini–Hochberg FDR over all tested ordered pairs, applied
   within each variant and window. That is the correct family: every pair is tested once.

**Verification.** Both are checked against reference implementations on the CT:
cross-correlations reproduce `pandas.Series.corr` on calendar-shifted series to 1e-10 for
every pair and lag tested; the HAC F statistics reproduce `statsmodels` OLS with
`cov_type="HAC", use_correction=True` to within 0.5% (`SETB01→SETG01` F = 3.8501 vs 3.8340;
`SAF115→SEFV` F = 5.2530 vs 5.2732; `SEHA→SEHC01` F = 0.7129 vs 0.7089). The small
difference is deliberate: statsmodels computes the HAC autocovariances on the *compacted*
sample after `dropna`, so it pairs 2025-09 with 2025-12 as if they were one month apart,
while P10 keeps calendar positions and zeroes the gap months.

### Stability filter

Everything is recomputed on four overlapping ~15-year windows and on the full sample. A
pair **passes** a window if |ρ<sub>best</sub>| > 0.25 **or** FDR *q* < 0.05 there. An edge
is **stable** iff it passes in **≥ 3 of the 4 windows**, with the **same sign** of ρ across
the passing windows and every best lag within **±2 months of their median**. Windows are
demeaned/residualised separately; lags are taken inside the window slab, so each window
loses its first 12 dependent-variable months. A node needs 80% month coverage inside a
window to take part in it (this is why w1 tests 4,970 pairs rather than 5,402 — three nodes
begin after 2000).

Reported ρ, best lag and *q* are **full-sample**; `n_windows_pass` and `stable` come from
the four windows.

Per window (`common_removed`): w1 2000–2014, 4,970 tested → 207 ρ / 118 FDR / 300 either;
w2 2004–2018 → 311 / 99 / 371; w3 2008–2022 → 552 / 242 / 696; w4 2011–2026 → 370 / 224 /

Note that 20 of the 70 stable edges do **not** clear the screen on the full sample: they
pass in three windows but wash out when the whole 26 years are pooled. Filter on
`stable AND abs(rho) > 0.25` if you want only the edges that are strong everywhere.

### Tables

```
p10_edges(variant, src, dst, best_lag, rho, fdr_q, n_windows_pass, stable)
```
**All** 5,402 ordered pairs per variant (10,804 rows), so the stage counts above are
reproducible in SQL. `stable = 1` is the graph.

```
p10_nodes(variant, item_code, name, weight, in_deg, out_deg, upstreamness)
```
`in_deg`/`out_deg` count stable edges; **upstreamness** = Σ|ρ| over stable out-edges −
Σ|ρ| over stable in-edges. Positive = tends to move before its neighbours *in this map*.
Top of the `common_removed` ranking: rent +0.81, meat/poultry/fish/eggs +0.51, pets and pet
products +0.39, nonalcoholic beverages +0.36. Bottom: food away from home −1.12 (8 arrows
in, and it is the single most-followed item in the CPI), fuel oil −0.72, college tuition
−0.46, appliances −0.45. 37 of 74 nodes have no stable edge at all.

```
p10_lagprofile(variant, src, dst, lag, rho)
```
Full-sample ρ at all 12 lags for the stable edges only (1,896 rows). Use it to check
whether a "3-month lag" is a peak or a plateau — a flat profile is a co-movement, not a
lead.

```
p10_meta(key, value)
```
`params`, `stages`, `sanity`, `run_at`, JSON-encoded.

### `out/p10_network.json`

Schema is documented at the top of `p10_network.py`. 159 KB. Contains, per variant: nodes
with major-group colours, stable edges (with `rho_other`), stage counts, the 15 strongest
edges, per-node 2-level BFS cascade with cumulative lag (25 nodes have one), and the full
12-lag profile of every stable edge. Plus the four windows, the eight group colours, the
sanity table, and the caveat string
`"lead-lag correlation, not pass-through, not causal"`.

### Known limitations

- **It is not causal and cannot be made causal by any filter here.** The stability rule
  removes accidents, not confounders. A shared driver produces exactly the same picture as
  a pass-through.
- **The screen is a disjunction, so this is not an "FDR-controlled network."** A pair is
  kept if |ρ| > 0.25 *or* its Granger FDR *q* < 0.05, so most displayed edges qualify on
  correlation strength alone and have not passed the FDR test — the majority of stable edges
  have full-sample *q* > 0.05. The primary false-positive control is the cross-window
  stability rule, not FDR; *q* is reported per edge so you can see which edges are also
  Granger-significant (many are not).

  **Why the OR is kept rather than tightened to an AND (measured tradeoff).** The disjunction is
  a real weakness: because ~2/3 of stable edges clear only the |ρ| screen, the graph admits
  edges that are correlated but not Granger-informative — spurious lead-lag on autocorrelated
  series. The `motor-vehicle parts → cereals and bakery` arrow (lag 5, ρ +0.37, 4/4 windows) is
  the kind of link this lets through: strong and stable in correlation, with no plausible
  mechanism, almost certainly two persistent series drifting together. The obvious tightening —
  require **Granger FDR *q* < 0.05 AND |ρ| > 0.25** in each window instead of OR — was tested by
  an independent read-only recompute against the live panel (CPI through 2026-08, same
  `hybrid`/74-node universe). It **collapses the network**: the main `common_removed` view falls
  from **70 stable edges to 2**, and `raw` from **91 to 11**. Two edges is not a network, so the
  AND screen is **not** adopted; the OR is retained and the cross-window stability rule (≥3 of 4
  windows, same sign, lag ±2) remains the real false-positive guard. The consequence to keep in
  mind: correlation-only edges (high ρ, high *q*) are the weakest class in the graph and should
  be read as co-movement, not lead-lag — filter the `p10_edges` table on
  `stable = 1 AND fdr_q < 0.05` when you want only the Granger-informative subset. Per-window
  pass counts under the AND screen were 25/39/98/79 (`common_removed`) and 30/37/173/131
  (`raw`) — strict enough that almost nothing clears it in ≥3 windows with consistent sign and
  lag, which is why the collapse to 2 edges is not a fluke of one window.
- **Persistent series correlate at every lag.** Rent, OER and food away from home have
  AR-heavy monthly rates, so any pair of them shows high ρ at all twelve lags and "best
  lag = 1" is close to arbitrary. This is why the Granger *q* is reported next to ρ, and
  why rent → OER (ρ 0.80, q 0.78) should be read as "same series offset", not "prediction".
- **The best lag is not sharply identified.** Neighbouring lags usually differ by a few
  hundredths. The ±2-month stability tolerance reflects that, and `p10_lagprofile` shows it.
- **The common factor is a single aggregate.** Leave-one-out fixes the own-item bias but
  the headline is still ~2/3 energy in its monthly variance (see P2: gasoline alone is 74%
  of the 24-month variance of headline SA MoM), so "removing the common factor" is closer
  to "removing energy" than to removing a clean latent factor. Three residual gasoline
  edges survive; `rho_other` flags them.
- **Sampling overlap inside a major group.** Nodes are non-overlapping in the aggregation
  hierarchy, but items in the same group are often collected from the same outlets by the
  same collectors with related seasonal factors. The apparel and pet clusters should be
  read with that in mind.
- **Uneven group coverage.** Housing has 15 nodes and 42% of the basket; "other goods and
  services" has 2 nodes and 1.1%. Absence of an arrow into a thin group is weak evidence.
- **Weights before 2018 are back-filled**, so the leave-one-out correction for 2000–2017
  uses the Dec-2017 relative importances. The correction is a small scalar adjustment and
  this is second-order, but it is an approximation.
- **October 2025 has no CPI.** Every lag is calendar-aligned and both 2025-10 and 2025-11
  are dropped by the coverage gate rather than shifted away. The headline regressor is
  mean-imputed for those two months (2 of 319 values) so the projection stays defined for
  the months after the gap.
- **The graph will change when it is re-run.** Adding one month to a 319-month sample moves
  ρ by very little, but edges sitting on the ρ = 0.25 boundary or on 3-of-4 windows can flip
  in and out. Treat individual weak edges as provisional; the food chain and rent/OER links
  are the robust part.

### Re-running

```
$CPI_ROOT/p10_network.py [--since=YYYY-MM] [--universe=hybrid|fine|coarse] [--rho-min=0.25]
                        [--fdr=0.05] [--min-windows=3] [--max-lag=12] [--granger-lags=6]
                        [--factor-lags=0] [--no-loo]
```

Idempotent — all three tables are rebuilt from scratch. **9 seconds** end to end on the pipeline host
(the whole point of the closed-form cross-correlation and the batched HAC F-test). Exit
code 2 means fewer than two sanity expectations came out stable in the main variant; the
tables and JSON are still written.

Depends only on `cpi_item_month`, so it needs to run **only when P0 has loaded new data**.
